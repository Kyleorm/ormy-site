ADHD object permanence: why out of sight means out of mind

Why you forget the food in the fridge, the bill in the drawer and the friend you meant to text, and what actually helps. Sourced, from someone with ADHD.

By Kyle Ormesher, founder of OrmyUpdated 30 October 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer

"Object permanence" is the name people with ADHD use for forgetting things they can't see. It isn't a real ADHD symptom, and you do know things still exist when you can't see them. What's actually going on is weaker working memory and attention, so nothing brings the thing back to mind. The fix is to stop relying on your head: keep things in sight, and use reminders that come to you.

What does object permanence mean with ADHD?

The term comes from child development. It's a baby learning that things still exist when they're hidden, which usually happens at around 8 to 12 months.

Online, people with ADHD borrowed it for something else: the thing you can't see stops existing in your head. For a lot of us, that looks like:

  • Finding food you forgot about at the back of the fridge
  • A bill in a drawer that you only remember when the late letter comes
  • Clothes left in the washing machine for two days
  • A friend you love and haven't texted back in a month
  • Buying something you already own, because it was in a cupboard

Is object permanence a real ADHD symptom?

No, not in the literal sense. Talkiatry, a psychiatry provider, says plainly that it isn't a symptom of ADHD. Healthline says there's no research showing people with ADHD have trouble developing it.

You know your friend still exists. You know there's food in the fridge. The knowledge is there. It just doesn't come to mind when you need it.

So the feeling is real, but the name is a nickname. A better one is simply "out of sight, out of mind".

Why does ADHD make things out of sight, out of mind?

The sources point to working memory and attention, and those are real and well studied.

Working memory is weaker. It's your brain's sticky note. A review of 38 studies found that adults with ADHD do worse on working memory tests, on average. When something leaves your attention, it can leave your head with it.

Nothing brings it back. Talkiatry describes it as trouble remembering things without a sensory cue. If there's nothing to see, hear or trip over, there's nothing to set the memory off.

Remembering at the right moment is harder. In one small study, adults with ADHD were much worse at remembering to do something at a set time. When the task was tied to something happening in front of them, they did no worse than anyone else. So don't read too much into one study, but it fits: a cue in front of you does what your memory won't.

How do you deal with out of sight, out of mind?

ADHD researcher Russell Barkley says help has to be at the "point of performance": the place and time you need to act. Everything below is a version of that.

  1. Keep what matters where you can see it. Open shelves and see-through tubs beat cupboards and drawers. A whiteboard by the door beats a list in your head.
  2. Put it in your way. The parcel goes in front of the door. Your tablets go next to your toothbrush. If you have to step over it, you can't forget it.
  3. Give the things you lose a home. One hook or bowl for your keys and wallet, every time.
  4. Set the washing-machine alarm when you press start. Anything that finishes out of sight needs something to call you back.
  5. Remember that a list is out of sight too. This is the one that catches people out. A to-do app only works if you remember to open it, and that's the exact problem you're trying to fix. Use reminders that come to you instead.
  6. Use reminders you can't lose by swiping. A normal notification goes off once. Swipe it away and it's out of sight again. For anything important, use one that keeps going until it's done. I compared them in persistent reminder apps.
  7. Get warned early about things far away. A birthday in three weeks is as out of sight as it gets. Set warnings a week or two before, not just on the day.
  8. Put people on the list too. A repeating reminder to text a friend might feel odd, but it doesn't mean you care less. It means they don't disappear.

Some people get on better with paper than a phone. Try both and keep what you actually use.

Could it be something else?

Yes. The NHS says memory problems can also come from things that are common and treatable, like stress, anxiety, depression and sleep problems. It says to see a GP if memory problems are affecting your day-to-day life. Go sooner if it's new, or getting worse. There's more on this in ADHD forgetfulness.

Questions people ask

Do people with ADHD lack object permanence? Not in the literal sense. That's a stage babies go through at around 8 to 12 months. People with ADHD know things still exist. The trouble is remembering them without something to prompt you.

Is object permanence an ADHD symptom? No. It isn't a formal symptom. It's a nickname for forgetting what you can't see, which comes from working memory and attention problems. Those are part of ADHD.

Why do I forget about food in my fridge? Because you can't see it, so nothing reminds you it's there. Move food that needs eating to the front at eye level, or use see-through tubs.

Why do I forget to text people back with ADHD? Once the message leaves your screen, nothing brings it back to mind. It doesn't mean you don't care. Reply straight away if you can, or set a reminder the moment you see it.

What's the difference between object permanence and object constancy? Object permanence is knowing a thing exists when you can't see it. Object constancy usually means keeping a steady feeling about someone when they aren't around, even after an argument. Neither one is an ADHD symptom.
